    L15 - Reflection and Transmission Coefficient Lab I#

    ECE 343 · Spring 2027

    Reflection and Transmission Coefficient Lab I

    Today you calibrate a network analyzer and measure loads whose reflection coefficients you can predict.

    Lesson 15 · Module 1: Foundations · Maj Ellis · Dr. Neil Rogers

    Learning Objectives

    1. I can measure the reflection coefficient across a range of frequencies and interpret the measurement using a network analyzer.

    Lessons 6 through 14 computed reflection coefficients on paper. This lab measures them. A vector network analyzer (VNA) sends a wave down a cable, measures the wave that comes back, and reports their ratio, \(\Gamma\), in magnitude and phase across a band of frequencies. Today you calibrate the instrument, which removes the effect of the cable and connectors, and then measure six loads: an open, a short, a capacitor, a matched \(50\ \Omega\) resistor, and two mismatches, a \(1\ \text{k}\Omega\) resistor and a complex load. You predict each one first, so every measurement is a check on the theory or on your setup. You work in a team of two or three at one station. Lesson 16 uses the same instrument to identify a device you cannot see.

    Where We Were

    Lesson 6: the reflection coefficient and the special loads

    \[\Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}\]

    Load

    \(\Gamma\)

    Matched

    \(0\)

    Short

    \(-1\), or \(1\angle 180^\circ\)

    Open

    \(+1\), or \(1\angle 0^\circ\)

    Lesson 6 derived the reflection coefficient from the boundary condition at the load, and Lessons 10 through 13 put it on the Smith chart, where the matched load is the center, the short is the left edge, and the open is the right edge. Every measurement today lands on one of those points or on a curve you can predict from them.

    What a VNA Measures

    • \(S_{11}\): the wave reflected back into port 1, over the wave sent out. With a load on port 1, \(S_{11} = \Gamma\).

    • \(S_{21}\): the wave arriving at port 2, over the wave sent from port 1. This is a transmission coefficient.

    A VNA describes a device by its scattering parameters, or S-parameters, which are ratios of waves rather than of voltages and currents. The subscripts name the receiving port and then the sending port, so \(S_{21}\) is the wave received at port 2 when port 1 transmits. With a single load connected to port 1, the reflected wave over the incident wave is exactly the reflection coefficient of Lesson 6, so \(S_{11} = \Gamma\). Lesson 16 uses \(S_{21}\) as well. The NanoVNA displays four traces at once: \(\vert S_{11}\vert\) in dB in yellow, \(\vert S_{21}\vert\) in dB in blue, the phase of \(S_{11}\) in magenta, and \(S_{11}\) on a Smith chart in green.

    Reflection in Decibels

    The VNA reports the magnitude as a voltage ratio in dB:

    \[\vert\Gamma\vert_\text{dB} = 20\log_{10}\vert\Gamma\vert\]

    \(\vert\Gamma\vert\)

    \(\vert\Gamma\vert_\text{dB}\)

    \(1\) (open, short)

    \(0\) dB

    \(0.905\) (\(1\ \text{k}\Omega\))

    \(-0.87\) dB

    \(0\) (matched)

    \(-\infty\) dB

    The analyzer measures wave amplitudes, which are voltages, so it converts the magnitude of \(\Gamma\) to decibels with \(20\log_{10}\), not \(10\log_{10}\). A load that reflects everything reads \(0\) dB. A perfect match reads \(-\infty\) dB, which no real measurement reaches: a good \(50\ \Omega\) resistor reads somewhere between \(-30\) and \(-50\) dB, and the floor is set by how well you calibrated. The \(1\ \text{k}\Omega\) value comes from \(\Gamma = \frac{1000 - 50}{1000 + 50} = 0.905\).

    Why Calibrate

    • Cables, adapters, and clips add delay and reflections.

    • Three known standards (open, short, \(50\ \Omega\)) let the VNA solve for that error and remove it.

    • Recalibrate after any change of cable or frequency range.

    Between the analyzer’s port and the load sit a cable, an SMA-to-BNC adapter, and a pair of clip leads. Lesson 7 showed that a length of line transforms the impedance at its end, so without correction the VNA would report the load as seen through all of that hardware, not the load itself. Calibration fixes this. You connect three loads whose reflection coefficients are known exactly, the open, the short, and the \(50\ \Omega\) load, at the exact point where the device will go. From the three measurements the VNA solves for the error the hardware introduces at each frequency, and it removes that error from every later measurement. The correction holds only for the hardware and the frequencies you calibrated with.

    Predicting a Capacitor

    For \(Z_L = -jX\), with \(X = \frac{1}{\omega C}\):

    \[\Gamma = \frac{-jX - Z_0}{-jX + Z_0} = \frac{X - jZ_0}{X + jZ_0} = e^{-j2\phi}, \qquad \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{Z_0}{X}\]

    \(\vert\Gamma\vert = 1\) at every frequency; only the phase changes.

    A capacitor stores energy and dissipates none, so Lesson 6 predicts \(\vert\Gamma\vert = 1\). To get the phase, we multiply the numerator and denominator by \(j\). The numerator becomes the complex conjugate of the denominator, so the ratio has magnitude one and a phase of minus twice the denominator’s angle. At low frequency \(X\) is large, \(\phi\) is small, and the capacitor looks like an open. As the frequency rises, \(X\) falls and the phase grows more negative, so on the Smith chart the measurement traces the outer circle clockwise from the open toward the short.

    Pre-Lab: The 470 pF Capacitor

    \(f\)

    \(Z_C\)

    \(\Gamma\)

    \(1\angle -0.8^\circ\)

    500 kHz

    \(-j677\ \Omega\)

    \(1\angle -8.4^\circ\)

    The packet asks you to fill in the expected impedance of the 470 pF capacitor at ten frequencies from 50 kHz to 5 MHz and plot them on the Smith chart before the lab. The table shows four of them, worked with the result of the previous frame. At 5 MHz, for example,

    \[X = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 5 \times 10^6 \times 470 \times 10^{-12}} = 67.7\ \Omega, \qquad \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{50}{67.7} = 36.4^\circ\]

    so \(\Gamma = 1\angle -72.9^\circ\). Work the other six the same way. Over the band you will measure, the capacitor covers only the upper quarter of the Smith chart’s outer circle; its reactance would equal \(50\ \Omega\) at 6.8 MHz, just above the top of the sweep.

    Pre-Lab: The Complex Load

    The \(50\ \Omega\) resistor and the 470 pF capacitor in series, at 2 MHz:

    \[Z_L = 50 - j169\ \Omega\]
    \[\Gamma = \frac{-j169}{100 - j169} = 0.861\angle -30.6^\circ = -1.30\ \text{dB}\]

    The second mismatched load puts a resistor and a capacitor in series, so it has both a real and an imaginary part. Its impedance at 2 MHz is the resistor plus the capacitor’s reactance from the previous frame. In the numerator the two \(50\ \Omega\) terms cancel, which leaves only the reactance. Dividing the magnitudes, \(169/196.6 = 0.861\), and subtracting the angles, \(-90^\circ - (-59.4^\circ) = -30.6^\circ\), gives \(\Gamma\). The resistance equals \(Z_0\) at every frequency, so on the Smith chart the trace rides the \(r = 1\) circle, starting near the open at 50 kHz and moving toward the center as the reactance shrinks. The packet asks you to work this before the lab and to explain the path in the lab.

    Equipment and Safety

    Each team of two or three cadets works at one station, and keeps it for both lab periods. The station has a NanoVNA with its SMA cables, a black SMA-to-BNC adapter, a BNC-to-grabber-clip cable, a \(50\ \Omega\) resistor, a \(1\ \text{k}\Omega\) resistor, and a 470 pF capacitor.

    • RF connectors are fragile. Tighten them finger tight, and never cross-thread one.

    • Pause the sweep before you change any connection.

    • No food or drink at the bench. At least two people must be in the room while equipment is in use.

    • Leave the bench as you found it: equipment in its original configuration, cables returned, trash removed.

    Procedure: Setup and Calibration

    1. Turn on the NanoVNA. Tap the screen, choose Display, and tap Flip Display so the ports face away from you. Tap Pause Sweep.

    2. Connect the SMA-to-BNC adapter to channel 0 (labeled “1” on the board), and the grabber-clip cable to the adapter. Connect the blue SMA cable to channel 1, and leave its other end free.

    3. Choose Stimulus and set the range from 50 kHz to 5 MHz with Start and Stop.

    4. Choose Cal, then Reset.

    5. Leave the clips apart (an open) and tap Open. Wait for the label to turn black.

    6. Clip the leads together (a short) and tap Short.

    7. Clip the leads across the \(50\ \Omega\) resistor and tap Load. Do not perform a Thru.

    8. Tap Done, save the calibration to slot 0 if you wish, and confirm that the analyzer highlights Correction.

    Procedure: Measurements

    Pause the sweep before each change, then restart it to measure. Read values from the markers at the top of the screen; move a marker by dragging it with a stylus or with Markers, Select Marker, and the side buttons.

    1. Open. Leave the clips apart. Record the magnitude and phase of \(S_{11}\), and where the trace sits on the Smith chart.

    2. Short. Clip the leads together and record the same quantities.

    3. Capacitor. Clip the 470 pF capacitor across the leads. At each frequency in the packet’s table, record the measured impedance, and compare the trace on the Smith chart with your predicted points.

    4. Matched load. Clip the \(50\ \Omega\) resistor across the leads. Record \(\vert S_{11}\vert\) in dB and the resistance the marker reports.

    5. Two mismatches. Clip the \(1\ \text{k}\Omega\) resistor across the leads, and record the magnitude and phase of \(S_{11}\) and the resistance. Then clip the \(50\ \Omega\) resistor and the capacitor in series across the leads. Record \(S_{11}\) and the impedance at 2 MHz, and watch the trace as the marker sweeps the band.

    For each load, write at least one observation comparing your measurement with your prediction.

    Where This Is Going

    • Lesson 16: recalibrate with SMA standards and identify a filter you cannot see from its \(S_{11}\) and \(S_{21}\).

    Today you measured loads you already understood, which tests the instrument and your calibration as much as the theory. Lesson 16 reverses the roles. The device under test is a filter with its label taped over, and you identify what it is, where its cutoff falls, and whether it looks capacitive or inductive in each part of its band, using only the analyzer.
